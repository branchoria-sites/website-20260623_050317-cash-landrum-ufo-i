## Introduction

One of the most important medical questions in the Cash-Landrum case is not whether the witnesses became ill, but how quickly they became ill. [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }}), [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}) and Colby Landrum all reported nausea and vomiting beginning the same night as the encounter, with later accounts often describing symptoms appearing within hours. That timing matters because acute radiation syndrome (ARS) follows dose-dependent patterns. Radiation experts frequently use the interval between exposure and the onset of vomiting as a rough indicator of how much radiation was absorbed. The shorter the interval, the larger the dose is generally presumed to be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">• Onset occurs 1 hour to 2 days after exposure. • Stage lasts for minutes to days. • Stem cells in...Read more...</span></span></span>

The result is a paradox at the centre of the Cash-Landrum [debate]({{ 'debate/' | relative_url }}). Early vomiting is not inconsistent with [radiation]({{ 'radiation/' | relative_url }}) exposure. ## What the Witnesses Said Happened First

Accounts of the incident generally agree on the broad sequence. After the reported encounter on the evening of 29 December 1980, Cash drove the Landrums home and then returned to her own house. The witnesses later reported that nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, weakness, burning eyes and a sunburn-like sensation developed that night rather than days later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

The exact timing is harder to pin down. Retellings often compress events into phrases such as “within hours” or “almost immediately afterwards”. Some later summaries describe Cash becoming ill shortly after arriving home, while the others reported symptoms the same evening. What is notable is that the sickness was not described as emerging after a prolonged symptom-free interval. The witnesses consistently placed the first gastrointestinal symptoms very close to the alleged exposure event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Spreaker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

This chronology became central to UFO investigators who argued that the illness resembled [radiation sickness]({{ 'radiation-claim/' | relative_url }}). In popular discussions of the case, the rapid appearance of nausea and vomiting was often presented as evidence supporting a radiation-related explanation rather than a conventional illness that happened to occur later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/cash-landrum-ufo-incident.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Stuff Works The Cash-Landrum UFO Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cash-Landrum UFO IncidentJanuary 1, 1970 — Vickie Landrum (left) and Betty Cash fell victim to radiation sickness after...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1970</span></span></span>

## How Acute Radiation Timing Is Usually Judged

Radiation medicine does not treat vomiting as a simple yes-or-no sign. Instead, clinicians pay close attention to when it begins.

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that nausea and vomiting are among the earliest symptoms of acute radiation syndrome and can appear from roughly one hour to two days after a significant whole-body exposure. The timing helps physicians estimate dose severity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">• Onset occurs 1 hour to 2 days after exposure. • Stage lasts for minutes to days. • Stem cells in...Read more...</span></span></span>

A well-established principle in radiobiology is that earlier vomiting generally indicates a larger absorbed dose. ## Why the Reported Timing Creates a Dose Problem

The difficulty for a radiation explanation is not that the reported vomiting occurred too soon to fit ARS. The problem is that it may have occurred so soon that it implies an extraordinarily large dose.

Medical literature on acute radiation syndrome shows that severe gastrointestinal illness appearing very rapidly is associated with exposure levels that are often life-threatening. The gastrointestinal form of ARS generally emerges at doses above roughly 6 Gy, while doses around 10 Gy or more are typically fatal despite treatment. Extremely early vomiting is one of the warning signs used to identify such exposures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41420-025-02525-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature+2NUEM Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gastrointestinal acute radiation syndrome: current...by ML Freeman · 2025 · Cited by 13 — Patients with GI-ARS experience a prodro...</span></span></span>

This issue has been raised repeatedly by critics of the radiation hypothesis. Discussions of the case by sceptical investigators note that if the witnesses truly experienced radiation-induced vomiting almost immediately after exposure, the corresponding dose estimate could be so high that survival for years afterwards would be difficult to reconcile with established radiation medicine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

The argument is not that radiation can never cause vomiting within hours. It certainly can. The argument is that the combination of very rapid symptoms, survival, and the absence of some expected catastrophic outcomes makes a straightforward high-dose radiation interpretation problematic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">• Onset occurs 1 hour to 2 days after exposure. • Stage lasts for minutes to days. • Stem cells in...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Could Vomiting Have Had Other Causes?

Nausea and vomiting are among the least specific symptoms in medicine. They can be triggered by many conditions unrelated to ionising radiation.

In the context of the Cash-Landrum incident, alternative possibilities proposed by commentators have included:

* Exposure to intense heat.
* Chemical or aerosol contamination.
* Psychological stress and fear.
* Dehydration or exhaustion.
