## Introduction

When the Cash-Landrum witnesses began seeking answers after their reported December 1980 encounter, they entered a very different government landscape from the one that had existed during the Project Blue Book era. The US Air Force had shut down Project Blue Book in December 1969 and formally ended its UFO-investigation programme after concluding that further study was not justified. As a result, there was no standing Air Force office in 1981 whose job was to collect, investigate, and archive civilian UFO reports. That institutional change profoundly shaped the later search for records. Instead of entering a dedicated investigative system that might have generated a [case file]({{ 'case-file/' | relative_url }}), the witnesses found themselves navigating claims offices, congressional referrals, and legal channels focused on [liability]({{ 'liability/' | relative_url }}) rather than unexplained aerial phenomena. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force+2National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unidentified flying objects and air force project blue book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Of a total of 12,618 sightings reported to Project Blue Book, 701 remained &quot;unidentified.&quot; The decision to discontinue UFO investigations...</span></span></span>

For the Cash-Landrum case, this mattered because one of the central questions became whether any military records existed that could identify the helicopters the witnesses reported seeing. The closure of Blue Book did not merely remove a UFO office; it altered where reports went, what records were created, and how officials searched for evidence.

## Why No Post-1969 Blue Book File Could Exist

A common assumption in later discussions of the case is that a government UFO file should exist somewhere. The institutional reality was more complicated.

Project Blue Book had served as the Air Force's formal mechanism for receiving and analysing UFO reports between 1952 and 1969. When the programme ended, the Air Force announced that UFO investigations would cease because previous studies had found no evidence that the phenomena represented a national-security threat or extraterrestrial technology. After termination, no successor organisation was created with comparable responsibilities. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force+2National Archives Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unidentified flying objects and air force project blue book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Of a total of 12,618 sightings reported to Project Blue Book, 701 remained &quot;unidentified.&quot; The decision to discontinue UFO investigations...</span></span></span>

The National Archives' Blue Book holdings therefore end with the programme itself. The archived case files, photographs, correspondence and investigative records belong to the 1947–1969 period. A sighting reported in 1980 could not generate a new Blue Book case because the system no longer existed. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives+2National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying ObjectsPro-UFO researchers claim that an extraterrestrial spacecraft and its ali...</span></span></span>

This seemingly simple fact had an important consequence for the Cash-Landrum [paper trail]({{ 'paper-trail/' | relative_url }}). Researchers looking for a missing UFO dossier are effectively searching for a record category that the Air Force had stopped creating more than a decade earlier. The absence of a Blue Book file is therefore not evidence that records were destroyed; it reflects a policy decision made in 1969.

## How Bergstrom Claims Staff Became the Entry Point

Because there was no dedicated UFO-investigation office, the witnesses were directed elsewhere.

After contacting elected officials, Betty Cash and Vickie Landrum were referred toward the Judge Advocate claims process at [Bergstrom Air Force]({{ 'bergstrom/' | relative_url }}) Base in Texas. The August 1981 [Bergstrom interview]({{ 'bergstrom-3cf9b0/' | relative_url }}) was conducted not by UFO analysts or intelligence officers but by legal and claims personnel. The transcript identifies Captain John Camp as Acting Staff Judge Advocate, Captain Terry Davis as Claims Officer, and Pat Wolf as Assistant Claims Officer. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/801229huffman_report3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">801229huffman report3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportTRANSCRIPT. of taped interview held at Bergstom Air Force Base Law Library Building 2102, 17 August 1981, between Betty Ca...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1981</span></span></span>

That detail is more significant than it first appears. Claims officers approach a report differently from a dedicated investigative programme. Their task is generally to determine whether the government may bear responsibility for alleged injuries or damages and whether a claim has legal merit. They are not primarily organised to build a comprehensive UFO case file, compare sightings nationally, preserve witness narratives for future study, or maintain a specialised archive of unexplained aerial events.

As a result, the documentary trail generated by the Cash-Landrum case followed the logic of a potential compensation claim. The witnesses' statements were recorded, correspondence was exchanged, and inquiries were made regarding possible military involvement. However, the administrative framework was centred on determining whether any identifiable government activity could be linked to the reported event. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/801229huffman_report3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">801229huffman report3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportTRANSCRIPT. of taped interview held at Bergstom Air Force Base Law Library Building 2102, 17 August 1981, between Betty Ca...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1981</span></span></span>

The record search therefore began from a legal question—"Were these government helicopters?"—rather than from a Blue Book-style question of "What was observed?"

## Why the Missing UFO-Investigation System Mattered

The closure of Blue Book affected not only where reports went but also what kind of records investigators later expected to find.

A dedicated UFO programme typically creates its own paperwork: case numbers, investigator reports, witness interviews, internal memoranda, analytical summaries, and correspondence. Blue Book generated thousands of such documents during its existence. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/project-blue-book-50th-anniversary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives+2Encyclopedia Britannica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">project blue book 50th anniversary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Each case file relates...Read more...</span></span></span>

In the Cash-Landrum case, no comparable investigative structure existed. Consequently, later researchers had to search indirectly through operational records rather than through a specialised UFO archive. The focus shifted toward ordinary military documentation such as [flight records]({{ 'flight-records/' | relative_url }}), unit logs, radar information, maintenance records, air-traffic observations and claims correspondence.

This created a practical problem. If the reported helicopters belonged to a military unit, evidence would have to be reconstructed from dispersed operational systems rather than retrieved from a central UFO file. If no operational records could be located, investigators were left with witness testimony and secondary inquiries rather than a purpose-built investigative dossier.

The institutional gap also affected expectations. During the Blue Book years, citizens reporting unusual aerial events could reasonably expect the Air Force to open a UFO case. By 1980, officials were operating under a policy environment in which UFO reports were no longer treated as a separate category requiring dedicated investigation. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force+2National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unidentified flying objects and air force project blue book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Of a total of 12,618 sightings reported to Project Blue Book, 701 remained &quot;unidentified.&quot; The decision to discontinue UFO investigations...</span></span></span>

## The Governance Legacy of the 1969 Decision

The importance of Project Blue Book's closure in the Cash-Landrum record search is not that it explains the incident itself. Rather, it explains why the search for documentation followed such an unusual path.

When the witnesses reported their experience, there was no standing federal UFO-investigation mechanism waiting to receive the report. The Air Force had already decided, more than a decade earlier, that such a programme was unnecessary.
