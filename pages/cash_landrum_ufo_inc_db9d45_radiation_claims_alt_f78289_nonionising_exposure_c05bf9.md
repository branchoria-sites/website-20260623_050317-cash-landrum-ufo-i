## Introduction

One of the most persistent debates in the Cash–Landrum incident is whether the witnesses’ illnesses were really caused by ionising [radiation]({{ 'radiation/' | relative_url }}) at all. As researchers examined the reported symptoms more closely, some concluded that the classic “[radiation sickness]({{ 'radiation-claim/' | relative_url }})” explanation created a serious problem: the level of ionising radiation required to produce the claimed combination of burns, vomiting, blistering and hair loss would likely have been fatal. That difficulty encouraged alternative proposals centred on intense heat, microwaves, radio-frequency energy, or a mixture of electromagnetic effects rather than a single high-dose radiation event. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

These non-ionising theories do not attempt to prove what [the object]({{ 'the-object/' | relative_url }}) was. Instead, they ask a narrower question: could a powerful electromagnetic source have produced some of the reported injuries without requiring the witnesses to survive a lethal whole-body dose of ionising radiation? The answer remains uncertain, but the microwave hypothesis became one of the most discussed alternatives because it offers a different mechanism for heat and tissue damage while avoiding some of the dose problems associated with conventional radiation-sickness interpretations. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[dia.mil+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalous Acute and Subacute Field Effects on Human</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">except by reference) discuss ionizing radiation tissue effects, unless they are adduced to be &quot;Mixed Field&quot;, e.g. the Cash-Landrum...Re...</span></span></span>

## Why Microwaves Entered the Discussion

The microwave theory emerged largely because critics and supporters alike recognised a mismatch between the reported symptoms and the consequences expected from severe ionising-radiation exposure.

Accounts of the incident describe intense external heat, painful skin effects, nausea, eye irritation and later [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}). However, some investigators argued that if these symptoms were caused by the level of ionising radiation commonly implied in UFO literature, the exposure should have been overwhelmingly lethal. This led researchers to explore whether another form of energy could explain at least part of the medical picture. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

Microwaves and other radio-frequency emissions became candidates because they can deposit energy directly into tissue through heating. Unlike ionising radiation, they do not damage cells primarily by knocking electrons out of atoms. Instead, they cause biological effects mainly through absorbed electromagnetic energy and resulting thermal stress. High-power microwave systems are known to produce burns and deep tissue heating under certain circumstances. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-ionizing_radiation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[ResearchGate+3Wikipedia+3Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Non-ionizing radiation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Non-ionizing radiation</span></span></span>

The idea also gained visibility in later discussions of unusual aerospace and defence technologies. A US Defence Intelligence Agency review examining alleged field effects associated with advanced aerospace concepts specifically referenced Cash–Landrum as an example sometimes discussed in connection with possible “[mixed field]({{ 'mixed-field/' | relative_url }})” exposures involving non-ionising electromagnetic energy rather than straightforward ionising radiation alone. The report did not validate the incident, but it illustrates how the case became part of broader conversations about high-power electromagnetic effects. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[dia.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalous Acute and Subacute Field Effects on Human</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">except by reference) discuss ionizing radiation tissue effects, unless they are adduced to be &quot;Mixed Field&quot;, e.g. the Cash-Landrum...Re...</span></span></span>

## What Symptoms Might Non-Ionising Exposure Explain?

A microwave or radio-frequency explanation is attractive to some analysts because it can account for certain reported effects without requiring a catastrophic radiation dose.

### Heat and Skin Injury

The witnesses repeatedly described overwhelming heat from the object. Microwave energy is fundamentally associated with heating tissue, and sufficiently powerful exposure can produce burns, redness and delayed skin injury. Medical literature on non-ionising radiation recognises that microwave and radio-frequency exposure can generate thermal damage, including erythema and burn-like lesions. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_burn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microwave burn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microwave burn</span></span></span>

This aspect fits one of the strongest elements of the testimony: the sensation that the object was radiating intense heat into the surrounding area. A thermal mechanism is easier to reconcile with reports of painful skin effects than some interpretations that require invisible ionising radiation acting without obvious heating. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

### Eye Irritation and Surface Tissue Effects

The witnesses reported burning eyes and irritation shortly after the encounter. High-intensity electromagnetic exposure can affect sensitive tissues such as the eyes, particularly because ocular structures dissipate heat poorly. Although the available medical records do not establish a microwave injury, proponents of the theory argue that eye symptoms fit more naturally within a thermal-exposure framework than within a severe acute-radiation-syndrome model. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

### Nausea and General Illness

One reason the [debate]({{ 'debate/' | relative_url }}) remains open is that nausea and weakness are not unique to radiation poisoning. They can occur after many forms of physiological stress, including intense heat exposure, toxic exposure, severe anxiety, infection and other medical conditions. Because these symptoms are non-specific, they do not by themselves distinguish between ionising and non-ionising mechanisms. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

## The Mixed-Field Idea

As discussion evolved, some writers stopped treating the choice as a simple radiation-versus-no-radiation question.

Instead, they proposed a mixed-field scenario in which witnesses might have encountered multiple environmental effects at once: radiant heat, electromagnetic energy, chemical contaminants, combustion products, or other emissions from an unknown source. Such models attempt to explain why the reported symptoms seem to overlap several categories of injury rather than matching a textbook example of any single one. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[dia.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalous Acute and Subacute Field Effects on Human</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">except by reference) discuss ionizing radiation tissue effects, unless they are adduced to be &quot;Mixed Field&quot;, e.g. the Cash-Landrum...Re...</span></span></span>

Within this framework, microwaves are not necessarily viewed as the sole cause. They become one possible component of a broader exposure environment that could have produced thermal stress and tissue irritation while other factors contributed to systemic symptoms. This approach gained attention partly because neither the traditional radiation-sickness explanation nor purely conventional medical explanations appear to resolve every reported feature of the case. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dia.mil/FOIA/FOIA-Electronic-Reading-Room/FileId/170026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.mil">[dia.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalous Acute and Subacute Field Effects on Human</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">except by reference) discuss ionizing radiation tissue effects, unless they are adduced to be &quot;Mixed Field&quot;, e.g. the Cash-Landrum...Re...</span></span></span>

## Where the Theory Still Lacks Proof

Although the microwave hypothesis avoids some of the problems associated with lethal ionising-radiation doses, it introduces its own evidential difficulties.

The largest problem is the absence of direct measurements. No verified microwave source was identified, no exposure levels were recorded, and no environmental data exist that would allow investigators to calculate how much electromagnetic energy the witnesses actually received. Without those measurements, the theory remains speculative. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>
